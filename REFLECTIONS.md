## Reflection 2: why the static feed isn't refreshed every 15 minutes
The static GTFS describes the *planned* timetable, which SETRAM only replaces every few months (the current weekday services run from 31 Aug to 30 Oct 2026), so re-downloading it every 15 minutes would produce the same output ~96 times a day. That's wasted compute and needless load on data.gouv.fr for zero new information.

## Reflection 3: auto-typed ID columns and joins
**Prediction:** if one file's `stop_id` is read as numbers and the other's as text, `1001` and `"1001"` aren't equal, so the join would match nothing (or crash), and every stop would end up with zero trips, possibly without any error.

**Compared to the script's comment:** the comment confirms this ("silent int64-vs-object merge failures"). Testing on the real feed showed a nuance. `stop_times.stop_id` became **float64** (blanks turn `1001` into `1001.0`), and `merge()` raised an error, but `isin()` (which this script uses) silently matched 0 of 214,507 stop-times. So the silent failure is real, just not where the comment says. Reading everything with `dtype=str` avoids it, because IDs are labels, not numbers.


## Reflection 4: per-stop vs. per-route logging volume
One snapshot from our pipeline had 4,635 stop-time updates, and 12 weeks of 15-minute snapshots is about 8,000 snapshots, so logging every update would mean roughly 37 million rows (~2 GB), too big for a git-tracked file. One row per route (26 routes) gives about 210,000 rows (~7 MB), roughly 180 times fewer. The cost is location: with per-route averages, a planner can't answer **"where along the route does the delay build up?"** (e.g. is route 5 late everywhere or only after one junction?), which is exactly what's needed to decide where a bus lane or traffic-light priority would help. The average also hides which specific stops are repeatedly skipped, and lets early and late stops cancel out.


## Reflection 5: alternative colour encoding for delay
The current rule paints early buses green, so in our snapshot route 33 (100 s early on average) looks like the best route on the network. But an early bus can be worse for a passenger than a late one: if it leaves before you arrive, you wait for the next one. I'd colour by distance from on-time instead: green inside a punctuality window, then a diverging scale with red for late and blue for early, darker the further from schedule. Because each bar is a route *average* (which already smooths out individual buses), I used a tighter window than the usual per-bus standard (−1/+3 min): ±1 min. A viewer would then see routes 33 and 23 as problems rather than successes, and the colour would point to different fixes: late (red) suggests infrastructure like bus lanes or signal priority, while early (blue) usually means a too-generous timetable that can be fixed cheaply by retiming the schedule. I applied this rule to the dashboard's delay chart.

## Reflection 6: static vs. real-time scheduling mismatch
My Reflection 2 guess covered the first half: the static timetable only changes every few months, so refreshing it every 15 minutes would regenerate identical output ~96 times a day. The real feed adds a nuance: the current weekday services end on 30 Oct, and our monthly job runs on the 1st, so if SETRAM publishes late, the reference stays stale for a month; the right cadence follows the source's publication rhythm (e.g. a weekly check that rebuilds only when the feed has changed), not the calendar. The other direction is worse: GTFS-RT only describes the current moment and keeps no history, so a monthly snapshot would capture one random instant per month (sometimes 3 a.m., sometimes rush hour). It would almost always miss short disruptions like route 5's 32-minute delay, couldn't show daily or weekly patterns, and anything missed is lost forever because there's no archive to go back to. The schedule has to match how fast the source changes, in both directions.

## Reflection 7: sanity check of one delayed route
I checked route 5 (Gazonfier ↔ Oasis), the most delayed route in every snapshot (+150–170 s on average, one trip at +1,911 s, 55–59 skipped stops). Looking at the raw feed, every route 5 trip skipped the same stops: Bollée, Marivaux and Guillaume Apollinaire, plus Comtes du Maine in one direction and Lazare Weiller in the other. SETRAM's traffic page (checked 21/10, 11:09) confirms all of them: roadworks on rue Alfred de Vigny and rue Gougeard since 12 October, and on Lazare Weiller (towards Oasis) since 20 October. So the delay is a real, planned detour, not a data artifact. The 32-minute maximum was a single trip that had disappeared from the feed by 11:11 (worst delay then: 12 min), so it was likely an isolated incident or a stale prediction, which I couldn't verify. I also found that the GTFS-RT alert feed reported 0 alerts during these works, so the dashboard showed "No active alerts" in green while route 5 was detouring. The alert feed can't be trusted as a complete picture of disruptions.

## Reflection 8: where population misleads as a demand proxy
Population counts where people sleep, not where they travel. Two concrete Le Mans examples: the **Campus Ribay** (Le Mans Université) and the **Centre Hospitalier du Mans** each generate thousands of daily trips from students, staff, patients and visitors who mostly live elsewhere in the métropole or the Sarthe, many of them without a car (students especially). Neither shows up in a population figure: they sit inside Le Mans' 148,876 residents, so a commune-level per-resident metric spreads their demand across the whole city instead of showing where it concentrates. The same logic applies to business parks and shopping areas, which bring in workers and shoppers but have few residents. Demand also depends on who lives there: a commune with many car-less households needs more service than one of the same size where most families own two cars, and the reverse makes car-dependent outer communes look "under-served" when few residents would actually take the bus. So the proxy is weakest exactly where it matters most: at major destinations and in transit-dependent neighbourhoods.

## Reflection 9: supply metric and a commune where it misleads
**Metric:** scheduled stop-visits per 1,000 residents, meaning how many times a bus stops in the commune on a representative weekday, divided by its population.

**Where it misleads:** our ranking puts La-Chapelle-Saint-Aubin first (1,010) and Coulaines second (762), while Le Mans, the hub of the whole network with the tram, comes only 10th (154). That's implausible. I checked each stop's real commune with the official geo API (geo.api.gouv.fr): 31 of La Chapelle's 53 stops, 80 of Coulaines' 105, and 19 of Rouillon's 41 are actually inside Le Mans. The script assigns each stop to the nearest commune *centre point*, and these small communes border Le Mans, so the edges of Le Mans are closer to their centres than to Le Mans' own centre. With only their own stops, La Chapelle drops to 454, Coulaines to 173 and Rouillon to 200, and Le Mans gains about 6,950 daily visits (≈200). Le Mans is still undercounted, because the "representative weekday" uses only the bus service: stops served only by tram T1, like Campus-Ribay and Hôpital, are missing entirely. So the ranking mostly reflects how stops were assigned and which service was picked, not how well residents are actually served. Assigning stops by real commune boundaries and including the tram service would be needed before using this to decide where to invest.

## Reflection 10: aggregating stop-level delay events
First, avoid logging the same event many times: in one of our snapshots, 3,623 of 4,523 stop updates (80%) were *predictions* for stops the bus hadn't reached yet, and the same trip/stop pair reappears in every snapshot until the bus passes. So I would record each (trip, stop) event only once, with its last observed delay after its time has passed, and skip any snapshot whose feed timestamp has already been logged (our history already contains one such duplicate). Then, instead of storing individual events, I'd keep one summary row per **stop × day × time band** (morning peak, midday, evening peak, evening): number of passages, average delay, number more than 3 min late, more than 1 min early, and skipped. With ~840 stops, 4 bands and 84 days, that's about 280,000 rows (~10 MB), the same order as the per-route log, yet it still answers "which stops are repeatedly delayed, and at what time of day". Keeping monthly files (as the pipeline already does) keeps each git-tracked file small.

## Reflection 11: what was answerable today vs. needs history
Reflection 1 was skipped, so there is no prediction to compare against; this answers the question directly, using the business question's two dimensions, **where** and **when**.
- **Answerable today:** which routes are delayed or disrupted *right now* (route 5's detour around three roadworks sites, confirmed by SETRAM), which stops have the most scheduled service (Gares, Mairie, Gare routière), and the network's overall punctuality at a given moment.
- **Needed judgment calls:** almost everything about "where to invest". The demand proxy (population, which misses destinations like the Campus Ribay and the hospital), how stops are assigned to communes (nearest centre vs. real boundaries, which moved ~6,950 daily visits), which timetable counts as a "representative weekday" (the script picked bus-only, dropping the tram), and how to encode delay (early is not "good", so ±1 min on-time window). Each of these changed the answer more than the data itself did.
- **Needs weeks of history:** everything about "when". Recurring patterns by hour and weekday, whether a route is chronically late or just disrupted this week (route 5 may be fine once the works end), school-term vs. holiday differences, and any prediction. GTFS-RT has no archive, so these only become answerable as our own 15-minute log accumulates, and only if the scheduled runs keep working.
